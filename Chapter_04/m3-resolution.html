Module 4.3 · General GIS principle · simple arithmetic

Cell size, extent and finding a cell

Four numbers in a header fix where every cell is: how many columns, how many rows, how big a cell is, and where one corner sits. From those you can compute the raster’s outline and find the cell under any point. And one warning that matters more than the arithmetic: a small cell size is not the same as accurate data.

In this module you will

  • Watch a pond turn into squares as the cell size changes — and a streetlight vanish.
  • Compute extent = origin + count × cell size, then find the cell for any (x, y).
  • See why two grids with the same cell size may still not line up.

4.3.1Cell size, resolution — and why neither is accuracy

Cell size (pixel size) is how wide one cell is on the ground — 100 m in our fixtures. Spatial resolution is the level of detail the grid can show, and it is set by the cell size: smaller cells, finer detail. Esri’s docs put it plainly: if a pixel covers 5 m × 5 m, “the resolution is 5 meters”. So cell size and resolution are one idea with two names.

The area is 400 m × 400 m (made-up). The streetlight is about 1 m wide; the pond about 150 m across.

Three consequences:

  1. Resolution decides what can exist in the raster. An object smaller than a cell is not represented — “if the pixel size is larger than the object of interest, that object may not exist in the raster dataset”. No styling brings the streetlight back.
  2. Resolution costs. Halve the cell size and you need four times as many cells for the same area. Higher resolution is not free and is not always wanted — the cloud map on the TV weather report is deliberately coarse.
  3. Cell size is not positional accuracy. A 1 m cell size says the grid is divided into 1 m squares. It does not say the values are placed within 1 m of where they belong. Accuracy comes from how the data was captured and georeferenced (Chapter 9), not from the grid spacing.
Everyday picture

Cell size is like the number of decimal places on a printed figure. 3.14159265 has more digits than 3.1 — but if the measurement was made with a school ruler, the extra digits are noise. More digits = finer precision; closer to the truth = accuracy. A finer grid gives finer precision only.

Common mistake

“This raster is 1 m resolution, so I can locate the pipe to 1 m.” A digging crew opens the road in the wrong place because a 1 m grid made from a 10 m survey was trusted at face value. Ask how the grid was made — a metadata question (Chapter 7).

4.3.2Metre-grid arithmetic: extent and finding a cell

The header of our text-file rasters carries four numbers: columns, rows, cell size, and the coordinate of the lower-left corner of the lower-left cell. This chapter’s fixtures have no coordinate system, so everything below is plain arithmetic on graph paper — it says nothing about the Earth.

Start with the blueprint’s own example: ten columns of ten-metre cells span one hundred metres. 10 × 10 m = 100 m. That is all “extent” is: count × cell size, added to the origin.

Try it

From a header to an extent

From a coordinate to a cell

Given a point (x, y) inside the extent, with origin (x₀, y₀), cell size c, and R rows:

  1. Column from the left, counting from 0: col = floor((x − x₀) ÷ c)
  2. Row from the bottom, counting from 0: rowFromBottom = floor((y − y₀) ÷ c)
  3. Row from the top: row = R − 1 − rowFromBottom — because grids are listed top-down while y grows upward.

Add 1 to each to get the “row 5, column 10” style used in this tutorial.

Try it

Type a point or pick a preset. The land-cover grid (origin (0, 0), cell 100 m, 10 rows) is used.

Boundary case — a point exactly on a cell corner

P1 at (200, 200) sits on the shared corner of four cells whose codes are 2, 1, NoData and 2. The formula above gives “row 8, column 3 → code 1 (built-up)” — but only because we chose floor, i.e. decided that a cell owns its left and bottom edges. Another program may decide differently. This is the raster twin of Chapter 1’s question about request P5 sitting exactly on the ward boundary: a point on an edge has no value until you state the tie-break rule, and that rule belongs to the software. The lab asks you to observe what your software does.

Common mistake

“Row 1 is at the bottom because y starts at 0 there.” Then every lookup is mirrored and DR-0042 lands in a vegetation cell. The listing’s first row is the top row; the header’s YLLCORNER is the bottom edge. Both are true at once.

4.3.3Same cell size, different grids

Two rasters with the same cell size are not necessarily on the same grid. A grid is fixed by its origin as well as its cell size. If one origin is shifted from the other by anything other than a whole number of cells, the cell edges do not coincide, and there is no cell in one that matches a cell in the other.

CaseExampleWhat it means
Same cell size, origins differ by a whole number of cellsLand cover (origin 0, 0) and elevation (origin 0, 600); 600 = 6 × 100The grids align. Cell-by-cell comparison is meaningful.
Same cell size, origin shifted by a fraction of a cellElevation and Rain_2026 at x-origin 50They do not align. Comparing “this cell with that cell” needs resampling (4.5), which changes values.
Different cell sizes100 m land cover and a 30 m classificationNeither lines up with the other; combining them needs a common cell size and origin — and the finer grid does not make the coarser one more detailed.

Both platforms have settings for this when rasters are processed together (ArcGIS Pro’s Cell Size, Extent and Snap Raster environments; QGIS’s GDAL Warp target resolution and extent) — that is later training. For now, learn to notice: origins of 0 and 50, or cell sizes of 100 and 30, will not line up, and any cell-to-cell comparison between them hides a resampling step.
