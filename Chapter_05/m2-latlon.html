Latitude and longitude: two angles on a round Earth

The most common way to say “where” on the whole Earth is with two angles, not two distances. This module shows what the angles are, how to write them, how to convert between the two common ways of writing them, and how to get the signs right.

In this module you will

  • Point to the equator, the prime meridian, a parallel and a meridian on a globe.
  • Convert degrees–minutes–seconds to decimal degrees and back — and check the answer.
  • Apply “north positive, east positive” as two separate checks.

5.2.1The globe: equator, prime meridian, angles

A geographic coordinate system locates a point with angles measured from the centre of the Earth. Esri’s ArcGIS Pro documentation puts it this way: locations are “defined using angular measurements, usually in decimal degrees, measuring degrees of longitude (x-coordinates) and degrees of latitude (y-coordinates)”. Move the sliders and watch the point and the two dials.

Try it

Equator

The circle midway between the poles. It is the line of zero latitude.

Prime meridian

The line of zero longitude. For most systems it runs “from the North pole to the South pole through Greenwich, England” (QGIS introduction).

Latitude

The angle north or south of the equator: 0° at the equator, 90° at the North Pole. Lines of equal latitude are parallels — they run east–west, like the rungs of a ladder.

Longitude

The angle east or west of the prime meridian, up to 180° each way. Lines of equal longitude are meridians — they run pole to pole and meet at the poles, like the segments of an orange.

The net of parallels and meridians together is called the graticule. A point is in one north–south hemisphere and one east–west hemisphere; the equator and the prime meridian are the boundary lines — just as request P5 sat exactly on the shared ward boundary in Chapter 1.

Why angles and not metres?

On a ball there is no flat sheet to lay a ruler on. An angle from the centre of the Earth is the same kind of measurement everywhere, so it makes a clean system for the whole world. The price: a degree is not a fixed distance on the ground in every direction — the next section shows why.

5.2.2Two ways of writing an angle — and one checked conversion

Angles are written in two common ways. Decimal degrees (DD): one signed decimal number, e.g. 23.0250. Degrees, minutes, seconds (DMS): like a clock — “There are sixty minutes in a degree, and sixty seconds in a minute (3600 seconds in a degree)” (QGIS introduction), e.g. 23° 01′ 30″ N. Notice that the example uses only four decimal places. More digits would suggest a precision the data does not have. How much is one decimal place worth on the ground? The QGIS introduction gives a fixed figure for the equator — “one second of latitude or longitude = 30.87624 meters” — and one degree of latitude is about 111 km. The GeoJSON standard (RFC 7946) says the same in another way: “6 decimal places … amounts to about 10 centimeters”. Move the slider.

Try it
Degrees are not metres — first reason

“At the equator, and only at the equator, the distance represented by one line of longitude is equal to the distance represented by one degree of latitude. As you move towards the poles, the distance between lines of longitude becomes progressively less” (QGIS introduction). At about 23° north (where set E5 lives) one degree of longitude is roughly 102 km, not 111 km: 111.32 × cos 23° ≈ 111.32 × 0.9205 ≈ 102.5 km. Same degree difference, different distance, depending on where you are and which axis you are on. (All these figures are rounded approximations on a sphere.)

5.2.3Signs: check north/south and east/west separately

In the usual signed convention, latitude is positive north of the equator and negative south; longitude is positive east of the prime meridian and negative west. Esri’s documentation states it the same way. Treat the two signs as two independent checks, because a mistake in one does not show up in the other.

Hand check of row 2: 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 → 12.5; 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25 → 45.25. Row 3: 52 ÷ 60 = 0.8667 and 12 ÷ 3600 = 0.0033 → 33.87; 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2 and 36 ÷ 3600 = 0.01 → 151.21.

“Usual” is deliberate. Some sources write hemisphere letters instead of signs (72.6 E); some GPS devices write west longitudes as positive numbers with a separate flag; some old survey tables count longitude west-positive. The CRS definition and the source’s own notes decide — the numbers do not.

Misconception

“If the number is between −90 and 90 it is a latitude; between −180 and 180 it is a longitude.” Every latitude is also inside the longitude range, and every longitude between −90 and 90 is also inside the latitude range. A range check can say a value could be a latitude; it cannot prove that it is one. Module 5.5 builds a whole exercise on this.
