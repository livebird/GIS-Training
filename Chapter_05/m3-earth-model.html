Module 5.3 · General GIS idea

The Earth model: shape, ellipsoid, datum

Latitude and longitude are angles measured on a model of the Earth. This module builds that model in three layers and shows what each layer adds — no geodesy (the science of measuring the Earth) beyond what you need to read a CRS record.

In this module you will

  • Say what the Earth’s shape, the ellipsoid and the datum each contribute, in that order.
  • See the same physical point get different numbers under different datums.
  • Explain why “WGS 84” names a datum, not a complete CRS.

Developer view

A row (id=17, amount=250) means one thing under schema v1 (amount in rupees) and another under v2 (amount in paise). Same numbers; the interpretation changed. A datum is the “schema version” of a latitude/longitude pair. Where the analogy stops: a schema migration is usually exact. A datum change is a physical re-measurement of the Earth, and converting between datums is approximate, with a stated accuracy (Chapter 6).

5.3.2“WGS 84” is a datum, not a complete CRS

Developers often say “the data is in WGS 84” as if that settled everything. It does not. WGS 84 is a datum (strictly, a datum ensemble — a family of very close versions). Many different CRSs are built on it, and they give completely different numbers for the same point. All rows below were read from the EPSG register.

Point G1 is latitude 23.0250, longitude 72.6000 in EPSG:4326. In EPSG:32643 the same point is an easting in the hundreds of thousands and a northing in the millions of metres; in EPSG:3857 it is two other numbers in the millions. All three are “WGS 84”. So when someone says “WGS 84”, ask: which CRS on WGS 84?

Misconception

“WGS 84 and EPSG:4326 are the same thing.” EPSG:4326 is one CRS whose datum is WGS 84. Saying “WGS 84” when you mean EPSG:4326 works only until you meet EPSG:3857 or a UTM zone — which are also on WGS 84.

5.3.3Where this chapter stops

“Ensemble”

The EPSG register describes the WGS 84 datum as an ensemble. That word signals that “WGS 84” has had several precise versions over the years, which differ from each other by decimetres to about a metre. For a city’s asset register this does not matter; for survey control it does.

Time-dependent frames

Continents drift by centimetres a year, so some modern reference frames need an epoch (a date) to be complete. This course deliberately leaves that out. If a project needs centimetre-level positions, the honest answer is “that needs a specialist”.
