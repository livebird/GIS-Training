2.1.1The five responsibilities

Chapter 1 said a GIS is a system — data, software, hardware, people — not one program. Here we split that system into five responsibilities. They are not products. They are jobs that something must do whenever geographic data travels from the person who makes it to the person who uses it. Click a box to read what it means for our town.

Why five and not one?

A developer new to GIS often expects one program to do everything, because a map viewer looks like the whole system. But each job has different owners, different ways of failing, and different rules:

  • Fixing a wrong ward boundary is a data-quality job (authoring).
  • Deciding who may see the request layer is a permissions job (content organisation).
  • Keeping the service that feeds a dashboard alive at 3 a.m. is an operations job — keeping servers running (service delivery and its machines).

Keeping them separate lets you ask precise questions later: “the app is slow — is it the app, the service, or the storage?”

General vs platform-specific

The five jobs are a general GIS idea: they hold for Esri, for QGIS + PostGIS + GeoServer, and for a Mapbox-based product (module 2.6). Which product does which job — and whether one product does several — is platform-specific.

Not every solution needs every box. An analyst who answers a one-off question on a desktop and emails a PDF uses only jobs 1 and 2. 2.1.3The phone is not the database

Authoritative data is the copy the office has decided is the source of truth. Think of the original register kept at the ward office: the photocopy a field worker carries is handy, but if the two disagree, the register wins. That is a decision, not a technical property: a file does not know it is authoritative.

Three things are true at once about that phone:

  1. It holds a copy of some data — at least what it has drawn; if the map was taken offline (downloaded for use without network), a local copy of the records.
  2. It is not where the authoritative data lives. Dropping the phone in a drain does not lose the city’s records.
  3. Its copy can be stale (out of date). If a colleague fixed a value ten minutes ago and the phone has not refreshed, it shows the old value.

The same is true of a laptop with a desktop GIS open, a browser tab with a dashboard, or a script that loaded features into memory. The application’s machine draws, caches and edits a view of the data; the storage system holds the data. And as 2.1.2 showed, publishing by copying can create a second copy — then the office must say which one is authoritative. The lab’s Solution S-2 makes that decision out loud.

Misconception

“I have the map, so I have the data.” Consequences: someone copies a project file to a colleague and the layers show as broken (module 2.2); someone assumes a public map may be downloaded and republished (module 2.3); someone tests against a cached copy (an old copy saved locally for speed) and releases a bug that only shows with live data.
