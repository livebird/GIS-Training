Module 1.3

Spatial thinking

Spatial questions come in six shapes. Knowing the shape tells you what data you need.

In this module you will

  • Recognise the six kinds of spatial question.
  • Tell absolute location from relative descriptions, and define the extent.
  • See why “nearest in a straight line” is not “quickest by road”.

Two things to notice. Connectivity needs a different kind of data — a network with links and junctions; lines that merely cross are not a network. And change through time needs history: a table that stores only the current status cannot answer the P5 question. In our fixture a free-text note is doing that job, which is a hint that a proper design (Chapter 8) would store status events as separate records.

Question types are query patterns: point lookup (location), GROUP BY area (distribution), a distance predicate (proximity), a membership predicate (containment), graph traversal (connectivity), an event log (change). The analogy holds well — except that spatial predicates carry geometric edge cases (a point exactly on a line) that ordinary equality does not.

1.3.2Absolute location, relative location, extent

Absolute location

A position in a coordinate system: DR-0042 is at (995, 510). You can compute with it — if the system is known (Chapter 5).

Relative location

“The drain beside the ward boundary on Main Road.” This is how most requests arrive. A GIS cannot compute with it until it is converted to a position, and the conversion always involves an assumption. Record the assumption.

Spatial extent

The area being considered. It is a decision, not a property of the data. Every count depends on it.

Try it

Is P6 in the study area?

Worked example. A caller reports “a blocked drain on Main Road near the Ward A/B border”. Which records could this be? Request P5 at (1000, 500) is a blocked drain; asset DR-0042 at (995, 510) is about 11 m away (5 m across, 10 m up; √(5² + 10²) ≈ 11.2 m). The GIS can show that a request already exists there. It cannot decide whether this call is the same blockage or a new one. That is a process decision — and it is exactly how P5 came to be “reopened”.

1.3.3Nearest by straight line versus quickest by road

“Nearest” is the most misused word in spatial work. Straight-line distance is a geometric fact. Travel distance depends on a network — roads, one-way rules, obstacles — and can be much longer.

Try it

Two requests, both 600 m away

A depot sits at (800, 200) inside Ward A. P2 at (800, 800) is 600 m due north. P1 at (200, 200) is 600 m due west. Now add a schematic canal (for illustration only) with a single bridge, and toggle the crew’s path.

Schematic obstacle and assumed path — not computed by any routing engine.

What to take from this:

  1. A straight-line distance is correct as a geometric answer and is often a good first filter (“candidates within 300 m”).
  2. It is not a travel time. A map shaded “within 300 m of the road” must not be labelled “reachable in five minutes”.
  3. Answering the travel question needs network data and a routing method — a later topic. Until then, say “straight-line” out loud whenever you mean it.
Misconception

“The request closest on the map is the one to send the crew to.” Only if closeness was the policy, and only if the crew can travel in a straight line. Both need checking.
