Module 1.3

Spatial thinking

Questions about “where” come in six shapes. Knowing the shape tells you what data you need.

In this module you will

  • Recognise the six kinds of spatial question.
  • Tell absolute location from relative descriptions, and define the extent.
  • See why “nearest in a straight line” is not “quickest by road”.

Two things to notice. Connectivity needs a different kind of data — a network where roads are joined at junctions; two lines that merely cross on the map are not joined. And change through time needs history: a table that stores only the current status cannot answer the P5 question. In our practice data a free-text note is doing that job, which is a hint that a proper design (Chapter 8) would store each status change as its own record.

Question types are query patterns: a lookup by key (location), GROUP BY area (distribution), a WHERE distance <= n condition (proximity), an “is inside” condition (containment), walking a graph (connectivity), an event log (change). The analogy holds well — except that spatial conditions have edge cases (a point exactly on a line) that ordinary = does not.

1.3.2Absolute location, relative location, extent

Absolute location

A position in a coordinate system: DR-0042 is at (995, 510). You can compute with it — if the system is known (Chapter 5).

Relative location

“The drain beside the ward boundary on Main Road.” “Opposite the temple.” “Near the bus stand.” This is how most complaints arrive. A GIS cannot compute with it until someone turns it into a position, and that step always involves a guess (which temple? which side?). Write the guess down.

Spatial extent

Simply: the area you are looking at. It is a decision you make, not something the data tells you. Every count depends on it.

Try it

Worked example. A caller rings the helpline: “blocked drain on Main Road near the Ward A/B border”. Which records could this be? Request P5 at (1000, 500) is a blocked drain; asset DR-0042 (the drain itself) at (995, 510) is about 11 m away (5 m across, 10 m up; √(5² + 10²) ≈ 11.2 m). The GIS can show that a request already exists there. It cannot decide whether this call is the same blockage or a new one. That is for the helpline process to decide — and it is exactly how P5 came to be “reopened”.

1.3.3Nearest by straight line versus quickest by road

“Nearest” is the most misused word in map work. Straight-line distance (“as the crow flies”) is a simple geometry fact. Travel distance depends on the road network — one-way streets, railway crossings, a canal with one bridge — and can be much longer.

The field team’s depot (their base) sits at (800, 200) inside Ward A. P2 at (800, 800) is 600 m straight north. P1 at (200, 200) is 600 m straight west. Now add a made-up canal (just for illustration) with a single bridge, and switch on the team’s driving path.

Made-up obstacle and an assumed path — not calculated by any routing software.

What to take from this:

  1. A straight-line distance is a correct geometry answer and is often a good first filter (“candidates within 300 m”).
  2. It is not a travel time. A map shaded “within 300 m of the road” must not be labelled “reachable in five minutes”.
  3. Answering the travel question needs road-network data and a routing method — a later topic. Until then, say “straight-line” out loud whenever you mean it.
Misconception

“The request closest on the map is the one to send the team to.” Only if closeness was the rule, and only if the team can travel in a straight line. Both need checking.
