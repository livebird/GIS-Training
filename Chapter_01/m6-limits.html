Module 1.6

Limits of a spatial conclusion

More reports is not the same as more problems. A beautiful map can still be wrong.

In this module you will

  • Think of other explanations before saying “this is the cause”.
  • Judge whether data is good enough for a specific job.
  • Spot three ways a convincing map can mislead.

1.6.1Many reports in one place does not mean many problems there

Suppose a map for the Commissioner shows many more requests in Ward A than in Ward B, and someone concludes “Ward A’s assets are in worse condition”. Here is a made-up monthly summary for the two wards. Change what the bars show.

Ward A has nearly three times as many reports, but per resident Ward B reports more. Ward A also has four times the streetlights. And 90% of A’s reports came through the app against 20% of B’s — a hint that A’s residents find reporting easier, not that A has more faults.

Before proposing a cause, list the alternatives

1. More people

More residents or passers-by, so more people who could report. Needs population data.

2. More reporting

More people use the app, an awareness drive was run, or one active resident reports everything. The Gentle Introduction’s health example records only patients who came for treatment; likewise our table records only people who chose to report.

3. More assets

More things there to fail. Needs an asset inventory.

4. Duplicates

Repeat reports were merged in one ward but not the other. Needs a check for duplicate reports.

5. Different time periods or boundaries

A ward boundary change moved records from one ward to the other. Needs the dates of each boundary version.

6. The condition really is worse

Possible — but only after 1–5 are checked.

The habit

“Compared with what?” must be answered before “why?” is attempted. Chapter 12 returns to rates (per 1,000 people, per asset) for map design; the point here is purely about reasoning.

1.6.2Fitness for purpose

A dataset is not “accurate” or “inaccurate” on its own. It is good enough or not good enough for a particular job.

JobWhat it needs from the dataIs our practice data good enough?
A city-overview map of where requests clusterPositions roughly right relative to wards; complete-enough coverageIn principle yes — but our practice data is a made-up grid with no real-world position, so good only for teaching
Assigning each request to a ward for reportingPositions correct relative to the boundary, plus a rule for edge cases like P5Yes for five records; P5 needs a rule, and the ward boundary must be the current version
Directing a crew to a fallen treePosition within tens of metresP6 vs TR-0301 differ by 22 m — probably fine for a tree
Finding a buried water pipe before digging with a JCBPosition accurate enough that the digger will not hit the pipe, from a proper surveyNo. A citizen’s tap on a phone map is not evidence of where a pipe lies. No number is given here because the required accuracy depends on the water board’s own standard

Remember this sentence: data good enough for a city overview may not be good enough for finding a pipe before digging. Neither how the map looks nor the number of decimal places in a coordinate tells you which case you are in. Only knowing where the data came from (1.4.3) does.

None of these are software faults, and no amount of careful map design fixes them. The defence is the validation step (1.2.3) and the four “who, when, why, what is missing” questions (1.4.3), asked before the map is trusted.

Misconception

“The map came from the official GIS, so the picture is right.” The GIS drew exactly what it was given. “Official” describes who published it, not whether it is current, complete, or suited to your question.
