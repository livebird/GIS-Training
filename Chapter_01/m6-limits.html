Module 1.6

Limits of a spatial conclusion

More reports is not the same as more problems. A beautiful map can still be wrong.

In this module you will

  • Offer alternative explanations before proposing a cause.
  • Judge whether data is fit for a specific purpose.
  • Spot three ways a convincing map can mislead.

1.6.1A concentration of reports is not yet a concentration of problems

Suppose a management map shows many more requests in Ward A than in Ward B, and someone concludes “Ward A’s assets are in worse condition”. Here is a synthetic monthly summary for the two wards. Change what the bars show.

Ward A has nearly three times as many reports, but per resident Ward B reports more. Ward A also has four times the streetlights. And 90% of A’s reports came through the app against 20% of B’s — a hint that A’s residents find reporting easier, not that A has more faults.

Before proposing a cause, list the alternatives

1. More people

More residents or passers-by (exposure). Needs population data.

2. More reporting

App adoption, a campaign, a vocal resident. The Gentle Introduction’s health example records only patients who came to be treated; the same is true of anyone who chose to report.

3. More assets

More things there to fail. Needs an asset inventory.

4. Duplicates

Merged in one ward, not the other. Needs a deduplication review.

5. Different windows or boundaries

A boundary change moved records between wards. Needs edition dates.

6. The condition really is worse

Possible — but only after 1–5 are checked.

The habit

“Compared with what?” must be answered before “why?” is attempted. Chapter 12 returns to rates and denominators for map design; the point here is purely logical.

1.6.2Fitness for purpose

A dataset is not “accurate” or “inaccurate” in the abstract. It is adequate or inadequate for a stated use.

UseWhat it needs from the dataIs the Chapter 1 fixture adequate?
A city-overview map of where requests clusterPositions roughly right relative to wards; complete-enough coverageConceptually yes — but the fixture is a schematic grid with no real-world position, so adequate only for teaching
Assigning each request to a ward for reportingPositions correct relative to the boundary, plus a rule for edge cases like P5Yes for five records; P5 needs a policy, and the boundary must be the current edition
Directing a crew to a fallen treePosition within tens of metresP6 vs TR-0301 differ by 22 m — probably fine for a tree
Locating a buried pipe before excavationPosition within a distance the excavator can safely work to, from a documented surveyNo. A citizen’s tap on a phone map is not evidence of where a pipe lies. No numeric accuracy is stated here because it depends on the utility’s own standard

Remember the formulation: a dataset sufficient for a city overview may be insufficient for locating a pipe during excavation. Neither the map’s appearance nor the number of decimal places in a coordinate tells you which case you are in. Only the data’s provenance (1.4.3) does.

None of these are software faults, and no amount of cartographic care fixes them. The defence is the validation step (1.2.3) and the four provenance questions (1.4.3), applied before the map is trusted.

Misconception

“The map came from the official GIS, so the picture is right.” The GIS drew exactly what it was given. “Official” describes who published it, not whether it is current, complete, or suited to your question.
