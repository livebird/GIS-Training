Module 1.2

What a GIS includes

A GIS is not one program. It is data, software, hardware, and people — doing four jobs.

In this module you will

  • Name the components of a GIS and its four functions.
  • Tell a GIS apart from a map image, a navigation app, and a table of addresses.
  • Learn the eight-step question-to-decision workflow used in the whole course.

1.2.1Components and functions

The QGIS Gentle Introduction to GIS says a GIS consists of digital data, computer hardware, and computer software, and adds that “GIS is more than just software, it refers to all aspects of managing and using digital geographical data”. Most courses, including this one, add a fourth part: the people and processes that collect, check, maintain, and read the data. That fourth part is added because most GIS problems come from people and process (nobody updated the data; nobody wrote down what a code means), not from the software.

Geographic data

Records that carry a location plus descriptive values.

In our town: requests, assets, roads, wards.
Developer: tables with a location column.

Software

Programs to view, edit, analyse, and output that data.

In our town: desktop GIS, web maps, field apps, your code.
Developer: apps, libraries, services.

Hardware

Machines that store, process, and display.

In our town: laptops, servers, the phones field teams carry.
Developer: infrastructure.

People and processes

Who collects, checks, updates, and reads the data — and the rules they follow.

In our town: helpline staff, inspectors, the person responsible for the data, the manager who sets the rules.
Developer: operations and ownership.

A GIS is a data platform, not one application: data comes in (capture), is stored, is queried and computed on (analysis), and is presented (communication), with an operations team around it.

Where the analogy stops: in an ordinary platform, a value like 250 means the same thing everywhere. In a GIS the system must also know how space is measured — the units, which map system the numbers belong to, and whether an edge counts. Two tables that look identical can give different distances because their coordinates were measured differently. Ordinary tabular data has nothing like that.

How a table becomes a layer

The Gentle Introduction shows a health worker’s table with longitude and latitude columns (the “geographical data”) and disease and date columns (the “non-geographical data”). Loaded into a GIS, the rows become a layer and patterns appear — for instance that “the mumps patients all live close to each other”. Our request table is the same idea: the x and y columns are the location; the rest describes the problem. Press the button.

Try it
Nothing about the rows changed. What changed is that the software now treats the x and y columns as space.

For a table to become GIS input, three things must be true:

  1. Each row has a location that can become a position. Coordinates are the direct case. A street address is not yet a position; converting it is a separate, error-prone step called geocoding (Chapter 9).
  2. Somebody knows what the numbers mean. Are they metres or degrees? Which map system do they belong to? Our practice data avoids this problem by saying up front “flat grid, metres”; real data cannot (Chapter 5).
  3. The other columns keep their meaning after import. Data types, empty values, and ID numbers must survive the load (Chapter 7).
Misconception

“A PDF map of the wards is our GIS data.” It is a picture. It cannot be filtered, its positions cannot be measured reliably, and it has none of the values that produced it. If the PDF is all that exists, the wards will have to be captured again.
