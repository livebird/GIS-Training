Module 1.2

What a GIS includes

A GIS is not one program. It is data, software, hardware, and people — doing four jobs.

In this module you will

  • Name the components of a GIS and its four functions.
  • Tell a GIS apart from a map image, a navigation app, and a table of addresses.
  • Learn the eight-step question-to-decision workflow used in the whole course.

1.2.1Components and functions

The QGIS Gentle Introduction to GIS says a GIS consists of digital data, computer hardware, and computer software, and adds that “GIS is more than just software, it refers to all aspects of managing and using digital geographical data”. Most courses, including this one, add a fourth part: the people and processes that collect, check, maintain, and read the data. That fourth part is added because most GIS failures are procedural, not technical.

Geographic data

Records that carry a location plus descriptive values.

Scenario: requests, assets, roads, wards.
Developer: tables with a location column.

Software

Programs to view, edit, analyse, and output that data.

Scenario: desktop GIS, web maps, field apps, your code.
Developer: apps, libraries, services.

Hardware

Machines that store, process, and display.

Scenario: laptops, servers, field phones.
Developer: infrastructure.

People and processes

Who captures, validates, maintains, interprets — and the rules they follow.

Scenario: call-centre staff, inspectors, data stewards, the policy owner.
Developer: operations, governance.

Developer view

A GIS is a data platform, not an application: ingestion (capture), storage, a query/compute layer (analysis), and presentation (communication), with an operations team around it.

Where the analogy stops: in an ordinary platform the compute layer works on values with a fixed meaning. In a GIS it must also know how space is measured — units, reference frame, boundary rules. Two identical-looking datasets can give different distances because they were measured differently. That has no counterpart in ordinary tabular data.

How a table becomes a layer

The Gentle Introduction shows a health worker’s table with longitude and latitude columns (“geographical data”) and disease and date columns (“non-geographical data”). Loaded into a GIS, the rows become a layer and patterns appear — for instance that “the mumps patients all live close to each other”. Our request table is the same idea. Press the button.

Try it
Nothing about the rows changed. What changed is that the software now treats the x and y columns as space.

For a table to become GIS input, three things must be true:

  1. Each row has a location that can become a position. Coordinates are the direct case. A street address is not yet a position; converting it is a separate, error-prone step called geocoding (Chapter 9).
  2. The positions are interpretable. Somebody must know the units and the reference frame. Our fixture avoids this by declaring itself a flat metre grid; real data cannot (Chapter 5).
  3. The descriptive columns keep their meaning after import. Types, empty values, and identifiers must survive (Chapter 7).
Misconception

“A PDF map of the wards is our GIS data.” It is a picture. It cannot be filtered, its positions cannot be measured reliably, and it has none of the values that produced it. If the PDF is all that exists, the wards will have to be captured again.
