Module 1.1

Start with a decision, not a map

A GIS answers exactly the question you ask it. So the first job is to make the question precise.

In this module you will

  • Separate the policy (rules people choose) from the evidence (facts a GIS can produce).
  • See how unclear words change the answer, using the six requests.
  • Compare the same records as a table and as a map.

1.1.1The recurring question

“Which unresolved service requests should an inspection team investigate first?”

This is a decision. Every decision has two parts, and people mix them up all the time:

Policy — the rules

The rules the office chooses. “High priority goes first.” “Requests older than ten days get escalated.” “A team stays in its own ward.” (Here “policy” just means the office’s own rules.)

A GIS cannot decide these. People with authority do.

Evidence — the facts

Facts the rules need. Where is each request? How far from the road? Which ward? How old? How many per area?

This is what a GIS produces, stores, and checks.

Developer view

Policy is the business logic. Evidence is the data the logic reads. A GIS is like a bank’s reporting database: it can show you the account balance and the unusual transactions, but it cannot approve the loan.

Where the analogy breaks: in a GIS the definitions of the evidence — what counts as “inside”, “near”, “the same place” — are geometric choices you must make on purpose. A financial SUM has one meaning. “Distance to a road” does not.

1.1.2Why unclear words give unclear answers

The policy usually says something like “investigate the urgent, unresolved requests near Main Road in our service area”. Each bold word changes the answer depending on a definition nobody has written down. Try it.

Try it

Where does “near” end?

Move the slider. The green band is the area within your chosen straight-line distance (“as the crow flies”) of Road R1. Notice what happens to P1 and P2, which are at exactly 300 m, and to P6, which is outside both wards.

How the distance is computed

Road R1 is a finite segment. For a point with 0 ≤ x ≤ 2000, distance = |y − 500|. Beyond the ends, distance is measured to the nearest end point. P6 at (2200, 500) is 200 m from the end at (2000, 500).

“Unresolved” — which statuses count?

The six requests use five different status values. Tick the ones you would call “unresolved” (still not fixed) and see which requests are in the list.

Try it

“Closed – duplicate” is the awkward one. It means two people reported the same problem, and this copy was closed. The record is closed, but P6’s fallen tree may still be lying there, open under the other ID — which is not in our practice data.

“Urgent”

Could mean Priority = High → P2, P3, P6. Or a type of problem such as Water leak. Or age — the oldest unresolved request is P2 (reported 28 August 2026). Three reasonable readings, three different lists.

“Service area”

Wards A and B only? Then P6 is out. Wards plus a 250 m margin? Then P6 is in. The GIS will apply either. It cannot choose.

The rule to remember

Before you ask the GIS to do anything, turn each unclear word into (a) a number you can measure, (b) a comparison rule that says whether the edge value counts (≤ or <), and (c) a unit (metres, days). This is true in ArcGIS, QGIS, PostGIS — every GIS.

Misconception

“The GIS will use a sensible default for near.” Tools do have defaults, but a default is not a decision. If you never looked at the default, you cannot explain the result to your manager, and a colleague using a different tool may get a different answer.

Two things to take away. The map is not always better: for counting, sorting, and exact values, a table wins, and many GIS tasks end with a table. But the map shows how records relate to each other — what is near what, what is inside what, which one is far from the rest — things the table hides inside its x and y columns. A GIS lets you switch between the two views and compute the relationships the map only suggests.
