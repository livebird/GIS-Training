Module 1.1

Start with a decision, not a map

A GIS answers exactly the question you ask it. So the first job is to make the question precise.

In this module you will

  • Separate the policy (rules people choose) from the evidence (facts a GIS can produce).
  • See how vague words change the answer, using the six requests.
  • Compare the same records as a table and as a map.

1.1.1The recurring question

“Which unresolved service requests should an inspection team investigate first?”

This is a decision. Every decision has two parts, and people mix them up all the time:

Policy

The rules the organisation chooses. “High priority goes first.” “Old requests get escalated.” “A team stays in its own ward.”

A GIS cannot decide these. People with authority do.

Evidence

Facts the rules need. Where is each request? How far from the road? Which ward? How old? How many per area?

This is what a GIS produces, stores, and checks.

Developer view

Policy is the business logic. Evidence is the data the logic reads. A GIS is like a reporting database: it can give you the balance and the outliers, but it cannot approve the loan.

Where the analogy breaks: in a GIS the definitions of the evidence — what counts as “inside”, “near”, “the same place” — are geometric choices you must make on purpose. A financial SUM has one meaning. “Distance to a road” does not.

1.1.2Why vague words give ambiguous results

The policy usually says something like “investigate the urgent, unresolved requests near Main Road in our service area”. Each bold word changes the answer depending on a definition nobody has written down. Try it.

Try it

Where does “near” end?

Move the slider. The green band is the area within your chosen straight-line distance of Road R1. Notice what happens to P1 and P2 at exactly 300 m, and to P6, which is outside both wards.

How the distance is computed

Road R1 is a finite segment. For a point with 0 ≤ x ≤ 2000, distance = |y − 500|. Beyond the ends, distance is measured to the nearest end point. P6 at (2200, 500) is 200 m from the end at (2000, 500).

“Unresolved” — which statuses count?

The six requests use five different status values. Tick the ones you would call “unresolved” and see who is in the set.

Try it

“Closed – duplicate” is the awkward one: the record is closed, but P6’s fallen tree may still be open under another ID that is not in this package.

“Urgent”

Could mean Priority = High → P2, P3, P6. Or a category such as Water leak. Or age — the oldest unresolved request is P2 (2026-08-28). Three defensible readings, three different lists.

“Service area”

Wards A and B only? Then P6 is out. Wards plus a 250 m margin? Then P6 is in. The GIS will apply either. It cannot choose.

The rule to remember

Before choosing any spatial operation, turn each vague word into (a) a measurable quantity, (b) a comparison rule that says whether the boundary value counts, and (c) a unit. This is true on every GIS platform.

Misconception

“The GIS will use a sensible default for near.” Tools do have defaults, but a default is not a decision. A result produced under an unexamined default cannot be explained to the decision-maker or reproduced by a colleague with a different tool.

1.1.3The same records as a table and as a map

Here are the six requests. Switch between the two views and think about which questions each one makes easy.

Try it
QuestionEasier in the tableEasier on the mapWhy
How many requests are High priority?✔Sort or filter. On the map you would read each label.
Which request is oldest?✔Dates are text; the map does not show them.
Which requests are close to Main Road?✔Visible at a glance. The table has no idea of “close”.
Is any request outside our wards?✔P6 stands out. In the table it is invisible.
Is any request on a ward boundary?✔ (with care)P5 looks “on the line”, but a drawn dot cannot prove it. The coordinates can.
Exactly how far is P3 from the road?Neither. This needs a computed value — the “analysis” part of a GIS.

Two things to take away. The map is not always better: for counting, sorting, and exact values, a table wins, and many GIS tasks end with a table. But the map reveals relationships between records — nearness, containment, outliers — that the table hides inside its coordinate columns. A GIS lets you switch between the two views and compute the relationships the map only suggests.
