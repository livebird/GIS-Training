Module 3.9

Recap, media notes, and what comes next

Six ideas to carry forward, one small 3D sketch, and the question that opens Chapter 4.

RecapSix things to remember

Self-test

Before opening each one, say it aloud in your own words.

1 · The real thing is not its shape

Point or polygon is a choice for a purpose and a scale; a richer shape is different information, not more truth. Asset, complaint and inspection are three collections. A feature is one row of shape + facts; it may be an invisible area (a ward) and may even have no shape yet (P7).

2 · Shapes are lists of vertices

Point = one vertex; line = vertices joined by segments; polygon = a closed ring. One shape type per dataset. A drawn stroke’s thickness says nothing about ground width — that is a column, or a separate polygon. X and Y are always there; Z and M are optional and formats disagree about them.

3 · Parts, holes, formats

A multipart feature is one row in several pieces (the park). A hole cuts area out (the depot courtyard) — a point in the hole is not inside. A closed line is not a polygon. WKT, GeoJSON and Esri JSON write the same ideas with different rules — GeoJSON outer rings run counter-clockwise, Esri’s clockwise.

4 · Attributes

Rows, columns, values; the map and the table are two views of one row. The business ID is not the system ObjectID and not the label. Colour by one column, filter by another, label by a third — none changes the data. Inspection history is a separate table linked by ID.

5 · Dataset, layer, map

A dataset is stored; a layer points at it with its own filter and colours; a map stacks layers. A filtered view stays live; an exported copy drifts. Layers higher in the list cover those below — check drawing order and filters before believing an empty-looking map.

6 · Inspect before you trust

Extent is a box, not a shape. Write the intake record: count, shape type, columns, rows with no shape. Predict which stored rows an action changes before you act: presentation changes touch none; edits touch exactly the rows you edit; edits sit in memory until you save them.

Audio

Describe every shape by walking its vertices aloud (“starts at five hundred, zero; straight up to five hundred, five hundred; then diagonally to eight hundred, nine hundred”). Introduce each unit once — metres, square metres, the printing point. Say ring directions as “right, up, left, down”. Never “this box” or “the red one”.

Transition to Chapter 4

Everything in this chapter was built from vertices: stored positions joined into points, lines and areas. Chapter 4 introduces the other fundamental way to store geographic data — a grid of cells, each holding a value, with no vertices at all — and asks what happens to “feature”, “attribute” and “extent” when the data is a picture of numbers.
