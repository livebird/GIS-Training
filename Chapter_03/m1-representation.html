Module 3.1 · General GIS idea

The real thing is not its shape on the map

A school is a school. On a map it can be a dot or an outline. Neither one is “the school” — each is a choice made for a job. This module teaches you to see that choice, and to keep three kinds of record apart: the thing, a complaint about it, and a visit to it.

In this module you will

  • Compare the same school stored as a point and as a building outline, and see which questions each can answer.
  • Keep an asset, an incident at it, and an inspection of it as three separate records.
  • Learn what a feature is — including features you cannot see on the ground, and features with no shape yet.

3.1.1One school, two shapes

Open any phone map app and zoom far out: your school or office is a small dot. Zoom right in: the same building becomes an outline you can walk around. The building did not change — the representation did. A representation is the shape (and the facts) we choose to store about a real thing.

In our practice town the school SC-01 is stored twice: as a point at (400, 700), and as a rectangle 60 m wide and 40 m tall (corners (370, 680) to (430, 720)). Which one is “right”? Wrong question. Ask instead: which questions can each one answer?

Rule to remember

A bigger, more detailed shape is not more true — it is different information. The outline knows the site area; the dot does not. But the outline still does not know where the gate is, because nobody captured it. Every shape holds only what was captured.

Developer view

Storing a customer as one geocoded pin versus storing the whole plot boundary: both are “the address”; one serves a delivery list, the other a property-tax map. Where the comparison stops: in a database you can add a column later without touching old ones. In GIS a layer holds one shape type (module 3.2), so “add the outline as well” means a second dataset, not an extra column.

3.1.2Asset, complaint, inspection — three records, never one

They must stay three separate collections. The asset and the complaint have their own point shapes. The inspection has no shape at all — it simply names the asset it is about (asset_id = DR-0042), and the asset supplies the location. If the drain’s position is corrected one day, the inspections follow automatically because they never held their own copy of the position. Chapter 8 makes this a formal relationship; for now, just keep them apart.

Misconception

“Put the latest inspection result in the asset row and you are done.” Then the second visit overwrites the first, and the trend — Fair in 2024, Poor in 2025 — is lost. That trend is exactly what a maintenance planner needs. The asset’s condition column is a handy summary; the inspections table is the record.

3.1.3What a feature is

A feature is one stored thing: one shape plus its facts, kept as one row. Drain DR-0042 is a feature. Ward A is a feature. Complaint P5 is a feature. Three points matter:

1 · Features need not be visible

A beginner’s book says a feature is “anything you can see on the landscape”. That is a good first picture, but too narrow. Ward A is a feature, and nobody can see a ward boundary on the ground. So is a PIN-code area, a “no-parking zone”, or a “300 m from the road” zone you compute later.

2 · A feature may have no shape

Complaint P7 was phoned in, but the caller could not say where. It is a real complaint with real facts and no location yet. Its row exists; its shape is empty. The GeoJSON format even has a word for this: an “unlocated” feature. Module 3.6 shows how to find such rows.

3 · One feature = one row

A feature has exactly one shape value (which may be in several pieces — module 3.3) and one set of facts. “Three drains” are three features, three rows.

Developer view

A feature is a row in a table that has one column of a special type — geometry — beside ordinary typed columns. ArcGIS names that column SHAPE; PostGIS uses a column of type geometry. Where the comparison stops: the geometry column has its own internal structure (vertices, parts, rings) and its own validity rules, and nearly every GIS engine forces every row in the table to have the same shape type.
